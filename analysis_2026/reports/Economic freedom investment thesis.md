# Markets Price Policy Direction Before Investors Can

The premise is half right about the world and mostly wrong as a trading rule. Western tax takes are at records (**the OECD average hit 34.1% of GDP in 2024**) and Europe's energy-intensive industry is shrinking, but on the two main economic-freedom indices most Western countries are flat or improving, and this repository's own panel shows the West's 2018-2023 decline (**−0.13 Fraser points, against −0.18 for the rest of the world**) sat entirely in the COVID year. As of October 2026 the best Western fits for "drifting away" are Canada (Fraser rank about 9th to 18th), the UK on taxes and energy costs, and Germany on heavy industry; the US is mixed, pairing permanent business tax cuts and deregulation with the highest tariffs since the 1930s and about $3.4 trillion of added deficits. Outside the West, Argentina, Chile, Vietnam and Nigeria lead the measured moves toward lighter taxes and more open markets, while Mexico, Indonesia and a band of tax-raising Central European and Central Asian states move the other way. Whether investing on this pays is where the thesis breaks. The repository's pre-registered 1997-2025 backtest failed all seven policy signals: sorting countries on improving economic freedom returned **−1.62% a year**, and on lighter tax burdens **−0.20% a year**. Markets price freedom gains in the year they happen and the year after (**+3.65% and +4.82% a year**), before the index publishes them, and only country momentum predicted returns robustly. Hand-picked reform episodes tell the same story: they beat emerging markets in about two-thirds of cases in year one but in only **five of eleven at five years**. The evidence supports at most a small, rules-based tilt that combines policy direction with momentum and valuation, screens out capital-control and expropriation risk, and uses US-domiciled ETFs held in a taxable account for the foreign tax credit. It does not support a large bet that published freedom scores, lower taxes or energy build-out will deliver excess returns.

## The Western drift shows up in tax takes and heavy industry, not in freedom scores

A note on sources: most 2025-2026 policy facts below come from search-result summaries of the cited pages, because direct fetching of Fraser, Heritage, OECD, World Bank and similar hosts was blocked during the research. Items marked "secondary" or "unverified" should be checked at the source before anyone acts on them.

### Taxes on labour and mobile wealth rose while taxes on investment fell

The tax leg of the premise has real support in the aggregate. The OECD average tax take reached **a record 34.1% of GDP in 2024**, up 0.3 points; 22 of 36 reporting countries rose, and labour taxes drove the increase ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/12/labour-taxes-drive-oecd-tax-revenues-to-record-high-in-2024.html)). The UK delivered two large tax-raising budgets. The October 2024 budget raised about £40bn, including employer National Insurance rising from 13.8% to 15% and capital-gains rates moving from 10%/20% to 18%/24% ([Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-autumn-budget-2024-what-uk-business-tax-measures-were-announced); [RSM UK](https://www.rsmuk.com/insights/budget/previous-budget-announcements/autumn-budget-2024-highlights)). The November 2025 budget added about £26bn by 2029-30 and froze income-tax thresholds to 2030-31, so the OBR now projects **an all-time-high tax take of 38% of GDP in 2030-31** ([Mortgage Solutions](https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/news/2025/11/26/autumn-budget-2025-millions-face-fiscal-drag-as-reeves-freezes-income-tax-thresholds/)). France, second-highest in the OECD at 43.5% of GDP, extended its surtax on the largest companies in a budget adopted in February 2026 ([KPMG Avocats](https://kpmg.com/av/fr/avocats/eclairages/2026/02/tax-measures-in-finance-act-2026-adopted-in-france.html)).

The sharpest moves target mobile wealth. The UK abolished the non-dom remittance basis from April 2025. Italy raised its flat tax for new high-net-worth residents from €200,000 to €300,000 a year ([Knight Frank](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2025/10/italy-flat-tax-2026-who-what-and-how-it-works)). Norway tightened its exit tax on unrealised share gains ([Schjødt](https://schjodt.com/news/the-norwegian-national-budget-for-2025)), and Spain runs a solidarity tax of up to 3.5% on net wealth above €3m, though whether it was extended into 2026 is unconfirmed ([Reuters via Malay Mail](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/2025/10/18/italy-to-raise-by-50pc-flat-tax-on-ultra-rich-to-300000-officials-say/195070)). Spending is the more durable channel. Finland (57.5% of GDP) and France (57.2%) had the EU's highest spending ratios in 2024 ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/government-at-a-glance-2025_70e14c6c/full-report/general-government-expenditures_395dfea8.html)). Germany's ratio rose from 45.2% of GDP in 2013 to 49.4% in 2024 ([Eurostat via DBnomics](https://db.nomics.world/Eurostat/tec00023)), and its March 2025 debt-brake reform and €500bn infrastructure fund will push it higher ([HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/energy/2025-posts/germanys-ambitious-fiscal-reforms-what-germanys-500b-plan-could-mean-for-infrastructure-investors)). The US One Big Beautiful Bill Act adds an estimated **$3.4 trillion to deficits over 2025-2034** ([CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/whats-one-big-beautiful-bill-act)).

The contradicting evidence is just as concrete. The average OECD statutory corporate tax rate fell from 32.3% in 2000 to 24.1% in 2025 and has stabilised rather than reversed ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/corporate-tax-revenues-rise-as-global-corporate-tax-rates-continue-to-stabilise-new-oecd-data-show.html)), and several Western governments cut business taxes in 2025-2026:

For an investor, "taxes on labour and mobile wealth up, taxes on corporate investment flat or down" describes the West better than "taxes up", and corporate investment is what equity holders own.

### Freedom indices show Canada and Germany slipping while most of the West holds

On the Fraser Institute's Economic Freedom of the World (EFW) index, the premise holds clearly for one country. The 2026 report, published on 6 October 2026 with 2024 data, drops **Canada from 9th to 18th**, the steepest one-year fall among advanced economies, driven by trade, regulation and government size; Canada now ranks 101st of 165 on size of government ([Fraser Canada release](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/sites/default/files/2026-10/economic-freedom-of-the-world-2026-newsrelease-cda_0.pdf); [The Hub](https://thehub.ca/2026/10/09/canada-drops-to-18th-in-2026-economic-freedom-rankings-as-economy-continues-to-struggle-report/)). Germany slipped from 15th to 19th. Over roughly a decade of editions, though, several Western countries moved the other way: from 2015 data to 2024 data the US rose from about 11th to 5th, France from 52nd to 39th, Switzerland from 4th to 2nd and Denmark from 9th to 7th, while the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands, Australia and New Zealand stayed roughly flat near the top ([Fraser 2017](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/studies/economic-freedom-of-the-world-2017-annual-report); [Fraser 2026](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/studies/economic-freedom-world-2026-annual-report); [Cato copy of EFW 2026](https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/2026-10/Economic-Freedom-of-the-World-2026.pdf)). These ranks are approximate because Fraser revises earlier years, and the 2026 figures come from the report's news releases and press coverage read through search summaries; the repository's own data mirror ends with the 2025 release ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)).

The two main indices disagree about the US. Heritage's 2026 Index shows the **largest single-year US gain since 2001, up 2.6 points to 72.8**, after a slide from 76.6 in 2020 to a record-low 70.1 in 2024 ([Heritage](https://www.heritage.org/markets-and-finance/commentary/good-news-the-us-economic-freedom); [Heritage 2025 US profile](https://static.heritage.org/index/pdf/2025/2025_indexofeconomicfreedom_united-states.pdf)). Fraser's preliminary estimate instead shows a **0.12-point US decline in 2025**, linked to larger government, weaker property rights and trade restrictions ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/practice-management/global-economic-freedom-edges-up-but-pandemic-damage-lingers/268479)). High-tax Nordic states, the Netherlands, Ireland and Switzerland all score above the US on Heritage 2026 because they rate well on rule of law, trade and regulation ([Heritage 2026 highlights](https://static.heritage.org/index/pdf/2026/2026_indexofeconomicfreedom_highlights.pdf)), which undercuts any blanket claim that Western Europe is becoming unfree.

The repository's own panel, built from the Fraser 2025 release and the full Heritage history, shows how narrow the drift is. From data year 2018 to 2023 the West fell 0.13 points, against 0.18 for the rest of the world, and the whole Western decline came in 2019-2020 (−0.25), followed by a partial recovery (+0.11) through 2023. After 2020, Norway, Sweden, Australia, New Zealand and Iceland kept falling, while Greece, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Spain, Belgium, Italy and Finland recovered the most. The West does lag where the premise says it should: on Fraser's government-consumption and top-marginal-tax components, on Heritage's government-spending score (**−4.5 for the West against 0.0 for other countries over 2021-2026**), and on electricity build-out, where 11 Western countries sit in the bottom third and four in the top. It does not lag on Heritage's tax-burden score, which improved for the West (+0.5, against −0.1 for others), and on the Fraser level 14 Western countries sit in the top third and none in the bottom ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)).

### Regulation eased in 2025-26 while US trade policy tightened

Regulation moved against the premise in 2025-2026. The White House regulatory office (OIRA) counts 646 deregulatory actions against five significant new regulatory actions in fiscal 2025; the claimed $211.8bn in cost savings is the administration's own figure and has not been independently audited ([OIRA](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaEO14192)). The EU's Omnibus I package narrowed its corporate sustainability-reporting rules to firms with more than 1,000 employees and €450m turnover, **exempting about 90% of companies previously in scope** ([PwC Luxembourg](https://www.pwc.lu/en/sustainable-finance/eu-finalises-agreement-on-omnibus.html)), and a separate deal delays the AI Act's high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). These changes roll back rules adopted in 2022-2024 rather than remove them, so the EU still has more regulation in 2026 than in 2015.

US trade policy moved the other way. The average effective tariff rate reached about **16% before the Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers tariffs on 20 February 2026, the highest since 1936**; without those tariffs the rate was 9.1%, still the highest since 1946 apart from 2025 ([Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/state-us-tariffs-february-20-2026)). Tariffs under Sections 122, 301 and 232 then restored part of the wall ([ST&R](https://www.strtrade.com/trade-news-resources/tariff-actions-resources/section-122-tariffs)). Fraser's framework counts tariffs as a loss of economic freedom even when they are framed as industrial policy, which is why the two indices disagree on the US.

### Energy costs, not regulation, drive Europe's industrial losses

The strongest support for the premise is Europe's energy-intensive industry, which faces a large cost gap with the US. The EU's average wholesale power price in 2025 was **roughly twice the US level** ([IEA](https://iea.org/reports/electricity-2026/prices)); since 2022, German electricity has averaged 2.4 times US prices and European gas 5.3 times ([Reuters via EnergyNow](https://energynow.com/2025/10/energy-transition-set-to-divide-manufacturers-across-the-atlantic/)); and the UK has the highest industrial electricity price among International Energy Agency countries, about four times the US level ([Institute of Economic Affairs](https://iea.org.uk/?p=44025)). The 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz widened the gap. European TTF gas traded just above €86/MWh in mid-October 2026, near a four-year high, with Brent around $104.5 ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/14/oil-prices-rise-after-reported-attacks-on-ships-in-strait-of-hormuz); [CEPR VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/energy-markets-under-chokepoint-stress-2026-hormuz-shock)), while US LNG exports passed 100 million tonnes in 2025, a record for any country ([Oil & Gas Journal](https://www.ogj.com/general-interest/economics-markets/news/55407251/us-lng-exports-on-track-to-top-120-million-tonnes-in-2026-energy-secretary-says)).

The output losses are lasting, not cyclical. German energy-intensive production **fell 15.2% between February 2022 and March 2026**, and total industrial output fell 9.5% ([Clean Energy Wire](https://www.cleanenergywire.org/news/energy-intensive-industries-germany-see-15-percent-production-fall-start-ukraine-war)). Announced European chemical closures reached 37 Mt since 2022, about 9% of capacity, and the industry body Cefic attributes **49% of closures mainly to energy costs and only 8% to regulation** ([SCI](https://soci.org/news/2026/1/chemical-plant-closures-surge-in-europe-investment-drops)). The UK lost Grangemouth's refining and Port Talbot's blast furnaces, Parliament took control of British Steel's Scunthorpe site ([The Chemical Engineer](https://www.thechemicalengineer.com/news/grangemouth-refining-to-stop-next-year-with-400-jobs-lost/); [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/industrials/british-steel-warns-2700-job-losses-from-blast-furnace-closures)), and the UK still bans new North Sea exploration licences ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/refined-products/030525-uk-plans-to-revamp-north-sea-windfall-tax-affirms-halt-on-new-licenses)). Policy is partly reversing: Germany's subsidised industrial power price, about 5 ct/kWh for energy-intensive firms, won EU approval in April 2026 ([PwC Germany](https://blogs.pwc.de/en/german-tax-and-legal-news/article/256673/industrial-electricity-price-things-are-finally-taking-shape/)), and Belgium repealed its nuclear phase-out law in May 2025 ([ANS](https://www.ans.org/news/2025-05-20/article-7054/nuclear-moratoriums-crumble-around-the-world/)).

The US is not the clean counter-example the thesis implies. Factory construction spending is about **32% below its 2024 peak** as semiconductor megaprojects finish ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/trump-said-d-rebuild-america-160508122.html)), and about $39.6bn of planned clean-energy factory investment has been cancelled since early 2025 ([EDF](https://www.edf.org/media/report-new-investments-continue-policy-changes-slow-us-clean-energy-manufacturing)). The ISM manufacturing index contracted for about ten straight months to 47.9 in December 2025, with respondents citing tariffs on imported inputs, before returning to 52.6 in January 2026 ([BusinessDay/Reuters](https://www.businessday.co.za/world/2026-01-05-us-factory-sector-slumps-to-14-month-low/); [Manufacturing Dive](https://www.manufacturingdive.com/news/pmi-ism-expands-january-2026-tariffs/811049/)). US residential power prices are up about 25.7% since 2022 on one preliminary, secondary analysis of EIA data ([startbusinessbystate](https://startbusinessbystate.com/data-center-electricity-prices-by-state/)). Manufacturing's share of GDP has been falling for decades in every rich economy, so the 2015-2026 decline continues a long trend, with a sharper break in European heavy industry after 2022.

The better framing for an investment thesis is divergence within the West, not a uniform drift. Small open economies such as Switzerland, Ireland, the Nordics, the Netherlands, Australia and New Zealand keep high, stable freedom scores even where tax levels are high. Large economies under fiscal stress, chiefly the UK and France, are raising taxes. Energy-importing industrial economies (Germany, the UK, the Netherlands, Italy) are losing heavy industry. The US combines lower business taxes and deregulation with protectionism and large deficits.

## Reformers cluster in South America, Vietnam and frontier markets as of October 2026

### Measured moves toward lighter taxes, open capital accounts and build-out

Argentina is the clearest measured case. Heritage's 2026 Index scores it 57.4, **up 3.2 points, the largest gain of any country**, though it still ranks 106th and is classed "mostly unfree" ([Heritage Argentina profile](https://static.heritage.org/index/pdf/2026/2026_indexofeconomicfreedom_argentina.pdf)). Fraser's 2026 report reportedly lifts its score from 4.35 to 4.86, the third-largest gain in the sample, mostly through the exchange-rate component; that figure comes from a secondary report because the Fraser PDF could not be fetched ([Economic Order](https://economicorder.substack.com/p/argentina-climbed-11-places-in-the)). A labour reform easing hiring and firing passed the Senate 42-28 on 27 February 2026 ([Friedrich Naumann Foundation](https://www.freiheit.org/ko/node/52402)). Companies can now send abroad dividends on audited 2025-and-later profits, which the IMF calls the first time in six years, while restrictions remain on older profits and the government aims to remove them by end-2026 ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/04/15/pr-26120-argentina-imf-reaches-agreement-on-2nd-rev-under-eff-arrangement); [US State Department](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/701264_2026-Argentina-Investment-Climate-Statement.pdf)). Crude output hit a record 861,380 barrels a day in December 2025 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/argentina-natural-resources-explained-2026/)), and the large-investment incentive regime (RIGI) has approved 23 projects worth $49.7bn, though these are plans and actual net inflows were only $762m through Q1 2026 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/argentina-vaca-muerta-rigi-deal-12-billion-caputo-2026/)). The risks are just as measurable. Inflation was still 33.5% year on year in August 2026 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/argentina-economy-2026-guide/)), MSCI kept Argentina in its "standalone" tier in June 2026 ([BA Times](https://www.batimes.com.ar/news/economy/msci-upgrades-bulgaria-to-frontier-vietnam-argentina-unchanged.phtml)), country risk (the sovereign bond spread) rose to about 636-655 basis points in early October 2026 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/argentina-markets-merval-peso-wednesday-october-7-2026/)), and a promised package to scrap about 20 taxes has not been confirmed as law.

Chile has the cleanest legislated tax cut. A reform passed Congress on 4 August 2026 that cuts the corporate rate from 27% to 23% by 2029, restores full crediting of corporate tax against shareholder tax, and removes the capital-gains tax ([US News/Reuters](https://www.usnews.com/news/world/articles/2026-08-04/chiles-congress-passes-kast-economic-reform-bill); [bne IntelliNews](https://www.intellinews.com/chile-s-kast-launches-40-measure-reform-package-with-staged-corporate-tax-cut-439180/)). The IMF, the World Bank and Chile's fiscal council warned that revenue losses outpace the spending cuts, opposition economists estimate a loss of about $4.4bn a year, and a constitutional challenge was filed against the law's tax-stability clause. Fraser 2026 ranks Chile 20th, up six places and first in Latin America ([UPI](https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2026/10/14/latam-chile-economic-freedom/8641791994472/)).

Vietnam fits the industrial build-out leg best. A May 2025 Politburo resolution names the private sector a "principal driver" of the economy ([VinaCapital](https://vinacapital.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/VinaCapital-Insights_Resolution-68_A-New-Dawn-for-Vietnams-Private-Sector.pdf)), GDP grew **9.01% in January-September 2026** ([VIR](https://vir.com.vn/vietnams-gdp-grows-over-9-per-cent-in-first-nine-months-162165.html)), and disbursed foreign direct investment reached a five-year high of $21.07bn ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/vietnam/foreign-direct-investment/news/589291)). FTSE Russell's upgrade to "secondary emerging" took effect on 21 September 2026 and phases in through September 2027 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/08/ftse-russell-confirms-vietnams-emerging-market-status.html)). Its Heritage score nonetheless fell 0.8 to 64.4 ([Heritage Vietnam profile](https://static.heritage.org/index/pdf/2026/2026_indexofeconomicfreedom_vietnam.pdf)); it remains a one-party state with foreign-ownership limits, and its goods trade swung to a $19.42bn deficit over nine months.

Several more countries moved toward the thesis on specific measures. Nigeria reportedly posted the largest Fraser gain of any country (a secondary report), unified its exchange rate so that the IMF now describes the FX market as "increasingly functioning on market fundamentals", and brought a new tax act into force in January 2026; but government revenue is only about 10% of GDP and the IMF still rates its debt-distress risk as moderate ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/06/09/pr26190-nigeria-imf-executive-board-concludes-2026-article-iv-consultation-with-nigeria); [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-0208-west-africa-from-legislation-to-action-2026-business-outlook-on-tax-reforms-in-nigeria-and-ghana)). Hungary's change is institutional: an April 2026 election produced a two-thirds majority committed to re-engaging with the EU, which could unlock €18-19bn of frozen funds ([Credendo](https://credendo.com/en/knowledge-hub/hungary-post-orban-era-suggests-political-reset-market-optimism-and-eu-re-engagement)). Bolivia removed most diesel subsidies, floated its currency after 15 years and agreed a $1.9bn IMF programme amid weeks of protests ([Bretton Woods Project](https://www.brettonwoodsproject.org/2026/10/imf-approves-programme-for-bolivia-as-opposition-to-linked-reforms-grows/)).

India passes the tax and energy tests but fails the freedom-level test. Income up to ₹12 lakh became tax-free in 2025, the goods-and-services tax was collapsed to two main rates, and the country added a record 55.3 GW of non-fossil power capacity in FY2025-26 ([News On AIR](https://www.newsonair.gov.in/budget-2025-nil-tax-slab-raised-to-%e2%82%b912-lakh-new-tax-regime-unveiled); [Enerdata](https://www.enerdata.net/publications/daily-energy-news/india-added-553-gw-non-fossil-power-capacity-fy-2025-26.html)), yet Fraser ranks it 88th and an unverified third-party table puts its Heritage score near 53 ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/practice-management/global-economic-freedom-edges-up-but-pandemic-damage-lingers/268479)). Saudi Arabia opened its main stock market to all foreign investors in February 2026 and is reviewing its 49% cap on foreign ownership ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/01/saudi-arabia-abolishes-qfi-status-and-opens-the-market-to-all-foreign-investors)), and South Korea now requires companies to cancel newly bought-back shares within a year ([Sodali](https://sodali.com/resources/insights/koreas-new-treasury-share-rules-and-what-they-mean-for-companies)). Colombia and Peru elected presidents in June 2026 who campaigned on a smaller state, more oil, gas and mining investment, and clearer rules for foreign capital ([HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/latamlaw/2026-posts/colombia-elects-abelardo-de-la-espriella-what-comes-next-for-its-economy); [MINING.COM](https://www.mining.com/fujimori-leads-peru-presidential-vote-as-mining-risks-rise/)), but in both countries this is stated intent, not enacted law, and Brazil's 25 October 2026 presidential runoff is still undecided ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/05/brazilian-stocks-jump-bolsonaro-now-heavy-favorite-to-win-presidency.html)).

### Measured moves away: state control, index exclusion and tax rises

The clearest movers away are Mexico and Indonesia. In Mexico, the US State Department lists the constitutional overhaul of the judiciary, the dissolution of independent energy regulators, Pemex's right of first refusal on new oil blocks with a minimum 40% stake in private partnerships, and the reservation of 54% of power generation for the state utility as sources of investor doubt ([State Department](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/mexico); [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/latamlaw/2025-posts/mexicos-energy-reform-a-return-to-state-control)). Headline foreign direct investment hit a record in H1 2026, but 88.5% of it was reinvested earnings and new investment fell 13.4% (secondary) ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/nearshoring-mexico-2026-guide/)). Indonesia expanded state-directed capital through its Danantara sovereign fund and raised the ceiling on pension and insurer equity holdings from 8% to 20% ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/03/27/indonesia-markets-msci-danantara-hormuz-iran-war/)). MSCI froze index changes in January 2026 over concerns about free float and ownership ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/01/30/indonesia-exchange-ceo-steps-down-as-msci-reform-pressure-mounts)), and on **16 October 2026 MSCI opened a consultation on cutting Indonesia to standalone status**; the Jakarta index fell as much as 11% that day, its worst day since January 1998, leaving it down nearly 40% for the year ([The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/business/business-news/2026/10/16/jakarta-stocks-plunge-11-as-msci-set-to-drop-indonesia-from-indexes)).

Brazil's current law raises taxes on capital through a new 10% withholding tax on dividends above R$50,000 a month, final for non-residents, and a minimum tax of up to 10% on incomes above R$600,000 ([EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2025-2232)); the runoff could change that direction. Several countries raised taxes without turning away from markets, mostly to pay for defence or close deficits. Kazakhstan raised VAT from 12% to 16% ([Sovos](https://sovos.com/regulatory-updates/vat/kazakhstan-adjusts-vat-rates/)); Romania raised VAT to 21% and dividend withholding from 10% to 16%, and Estonia and Lithuania raised corporate taxes ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/blog/europe-tax-trends-reform/)); and Poland is closing a record deficit of 7.3% of GDP mainly through revenue measures ([European Commission](https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/economic-surveillance-eu-member-states/country-pages/poland/economic-forecast-poland_en)). These countries are moving away on taxes but not on property rights or openness. The Gulf scores high on freedom levels, but the 2026 war has stalled reforms and growth, with the IMF projecting 2026 contractions of 15.6% in Qatar and 16.7% in Kuwait ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/13/middle-east-and-central-asias-economy-to-shrink-in-2026-amid-war-imf-says); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/amid-iran-war-gulf-countries-slow-the-pace-of-reforms)). China shows that industrial build-out is not the same as economic freedom: high-tech manufacturing grew 13.8% in January-August 2026 while Heritage ranks China 154th ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.com.my/2026/09/15/chinas-industrial-output-rises-5-3-from-jan-august-as-factory-activities-improve/); [Daily Signal](https://www.dailysignal.com/2026/03/10/heritage-index-credits-trump-administration-largest-economic-freedom-surge-25-years/)).

### Index data run one to two years behind the policy news

Neither index has caught up. The composite failed its pre-registered test, so the ranking is descriptive, not a buy list, but it shows that a mechanical index screen in October 2026 would point toward economies the energy shock has hurt and away from the country with the largest measured reform. ## Seven pre-registered tests failed because markets move before the data publish

### The academic prior was already weak

Before the repository ran its test, the published research already pointed toward no tradable effect. Stocker's 2005 Cato Journal study found the *rate of increase* in economic freedom related to equity returns while the *level* had no effect ([Stocker 2005](https://www.cato.org/sites/cato.org/files/serials/files/cato-journal/2005/11/cj25n3-14.pdf)), but most supportive "change" results measure the change over the same window as the returns, which an investor cannot trade. The robust finding for levels is lower volatility, not higher returns: Chen and Huang found adjusted returns "bear little relationship" to freedom ([Chen & Huang](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eme/ijaimp/v17y2009i2p189-197.html)), and Blau, Brough and Thomas found home-country freedom inversely related to price volatility across 327 ADRs ([Blau et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jimfin/v41y2014icp182-196.html)). A risk-based view points the other way on returns. Erb, Harvey and Viskanta found the highest-risk quartile of countries averaged about 27% a year, against 15% for the lowest ([Erb, Harvey & Viskanta](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/papers/p26.html)), and Hail and Leuz found that stronger institutions mean a lower cost of equity, which implies lower expected returns once the improvement is priced ([Hail & Leuz](https://www.ecgi.global/publications/working-papers/international-differences-in-the-cost-of-equity-capital-do-legal)).

Growth does not reliably reach shareholders either. Ritter reports cross-country correlations between real GDP growth and stock returns of **−0.39 for 19 developed markets (1900-2011) and −0.41 for 15 emerging markets** ([Ritter 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1745-6622.2012.00385.x)), and MSCI China returned about 2% a year over 1994-2013 while the economy grew far faster (a secondary source; the growth figure is probably nominal) ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/3db233e4-be13-4d4b-bd94-45e60dce224a)). Policy news is priced as a one-off jump. Stock markets earned about 3.3% a month in real dollars in the eight months *before* liberalisations ([Henry 2000](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v55y2000i2p529-564.html)); inflation stabilisations lifted stocks about 24% only when inflation had been above 40%, and about zero otherwise ([Henry 2002](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8289)); tax burdens show up as lower valuations ([Sialm 2009](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11756)); and prices fall on average when policy changes are announced ([Pástor & Veronesi 2012](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16128)). Pástor and Veronesi also find that US excess returns over 1927-2015 were higher under the party associated with *more* redistribution, which they attribute to risk premia rather than policy merit; with only about 22 presidential terms, the evidence cannot support a partisan tilt in either direction ([Pástor & Veronesi 2020](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23184)).

### The repository's backtest ruled out large edges

The repository pre-registered seven tests at commit b100171 before any results existed ([pre-registration](../FREEDOM_PREREG.md)). Returns are Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen (JKP) country stock-market returns in USD, covering all listed stocks and value-weighted. At each end-October formation date from 1997 to 2025, the universe is every country with at least 20 stocks, about 50 countries in 1997 and 75 in 2025; Venezuela and Zimbabwe are excluded for unreliable exchange rates, and Russia is marked −100% in March 2022. Each signal uses only data that was public at the formation date. The portfolio is long the top third of countries and short the bottom third, equal-weighted and held 12 months, and a signal passes only if its mean is positive, its Newey-West t-statistic is at least 2, it is positive in both halves of the sample, and it survives a Holm correction across the seven tests. An adversarial audit confirmed 34 findings, none of which changed a verdict ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)).

| Signal | Top minus bottom third, %/yr | t-statistic | One-sided 95% upper bound, %/yr | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1: 5-year change in Fraser economic freedom | −1.62 | −0.92 | 1.3 | Fail |
| S2: Fraser freedom level | −1.51 | −0.73 | 1.9 | Fail |
| S3: 5-year change in Heritage tax burden (lighter = higher) | −0.20 | −0.13 | 2.25 | Fail |
| S4: 5-year change in manufacturing share of GDP | +2.00 | +1.12 | 4.9 | Fail |
| S5: 5-year growth in electricity generation per person | −0.74 | −0.38 | 2.4 | Fail |
| S6: S1 among Western countries only | −0.38 | −0.26 | 2.0 | Fail |
| S7: composite of S1, S3, S4, S5 | +1.11 | +0.63 | 4.0 | Fail |

Source: [backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md); [summary.txt](../results_freedom/summary.txt).

A second check used Fama-MacBeth regressions that control for each country's prior 12-month return (momentum) and long-term reversal. No policy coefficient reached a t-statistic of 1.4 in absolute value, while **country momentum was the only robust predictor (t about 3)**, worth about 0.22-0.28% a month per standard deviation ([summary.txt](../results_freedom/summary.txt)). The test had 80% power only for premiums of about 4-6% a year. It rules out large edges for freedom changes, freedom levels, tax-burden changes, electricity growth and the Western-only test, whose 95% upper bounds are 1.3-2.4% a year; it can neither confirm nor exclude edges of 1-3% a year; and it leaves 4-5% a year open for manufacturing (S4) and the composite (S7). The first cohort, formed in October 1997 and held through the Asian and Russian crises, has the most influence. Without it the freedom-change signal is −0.4% a year (no edge), the freedom-level signal is −3.1% a year (t −1.8, so freer countries did somewhat worse), and manufacturing rises to +3.3% a year ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)).

Manufacturing is the only signal positive in nearly every variant, and it is fragile. It disappears in an MSCI ACWI-only universe (+1.1%, t 0.5), in markets with at least 50 stocks and under value weights, so it lives mostly in small and frontier markets. On iShares country ETFs over 2001-2025 it shows +3.1% a year (t 2.1, Holm p 0.10), but the JKP returns give the same number on the same country-years, so this reflects the sample period rather than independent confirmation. The World Bank data are today's revised figures rather than what was known at the time, and the signal also picks up sector-mix shifts, such as pharma and petrochemicals or GDP shrinking in a crisis, not just policy-driven build-out. The tax leg fared worse: among developed markets, countries whose measured tax burden lightened did **2.7% a year worse (t −2.3)**. That result is fragile, concentrated in 2000-2011 and one of more than 40 exploratory rows; it is not evidence that tax cuts hurt, but it gives the thesis no support ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)). The only version a US investor can actually build is long-only, overweighting the top third against all countries, and it returned −0.5% a year on freedom change, −0.7% on freedom level, +0.4% on tax burden, +1.0% on manufacturing and +0.8% on the composite, none statistically significant and all before fees and taxes.

### Freedom gains pay while they happen, not after they are published

An after-the-fact check explains the null. Sorting countries on the one-year change in Fraser freedom in data year d shows the relation is real but arrives early:

| Return year | Top minus bottom third, %/yr | t-statistic | What an investor could know |
|---|---|---|---|
| d (same year) | +3.65 | 2.7 | Change not yet measured |
| d+1 | +4.82 | 2.9 | Still before Fraser publishes, around September of d+2 |
| d+2 | +0.68 | 0.4 | Publication year |
| d+3 | −2.15 | −1.5 | Published |

Source: [timing_ladder.csv](../results_freedom/timing_ladder.csv); [backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md).

The entire premium accrues before an investor using the index could see it. Heritage tax-burden changes show no relation at any timing. Manufacturing-share changes earn +5.4% a year (t 3.3) in d+1, a window that straddles the World Bank's mid-year publication, and +3.4% (t 1.8) in d+2, a weak remnant that might be tradable. This matches Henry's run-ups before liberalisation, and a paper attributed to Stocker (attribution unverified) that says markets identify freedom gains "long before" the indices are published ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eme/jfeppp/v8y2016i2p248-267.html)). Policy direction is informative only to an investor who forecasts it before everyone else does, which is a forecasting skill, not a screen anyone can run on published data.

### Betting against the West's measured drift did not pay either

The global freedom-change sort leaned against the West: of 591 Western country-years, 271 landed in the short third and only 54 in the long third, and the US was in the short third in 20 of 29 years. Yet the tests aimed squarely at the premise were flat, at −0.4% a year for the Western-only sort and +0.35% a year for freedom changes within developed markets ([backtest notes](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/backtest_results.md)). The US, usually on the short side, was one of the period's strongest markets; from November 2016 to September 2026 SPY returned 13.5% a year, against 6.3% for developed markets outside North America (EFA), price-only ([Robinhood market data, 2026-10-16](https://robinhood.com/us/en/stocks/SPY/)). The test has limits that matter for this thesis. It covers only gradual, published policy drift in markets that stayed listed, so it cannot measure the value of avoiding catastrophes: Venezuela and Zimbabwe are excluded, Russia is the only collapse in the sample, and closed-market months such as Greece's in July-August 2015 are dropped. Its returns are also gross, pre-tax and long-short.

## Reform trades reward early movers, then fade within three to five years

### Hit rates fall from two-thirds at one year to under half at five

The repository's case studies cover 16 modern market-opening reform episodes with US-listed ETF data. Measured against EEM, the emerging-markets ETF, **about 11 of 16 outperformed in the first year, 7 of 13 at three years and 5 of 11 at five years** ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). The sample is hand-picked, small and overlapping, but the pattern is consistent. Much of the re-rating comes before the policy is obvious: Argentina's ETF beat EEM by 53% in the year before Milei's November 2023 runoff, India's by 25% from Modi's nomination to his May 2014 win, and Brazil's by 33% in the months before its 2018 election. Buying at the confirmation event still earned a first-year premium relative to EEM in about two-thirds of cases, including Milei (+51%), Macri (+17%), Japan's 2023 stock-exchange push on capital efficiency (+16%) and India (+11%) ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)).

Reversals are common. Argentina's ETF doubled from Macri's November 2015 election to January 2018 (+102%, against EEM's +50%), then fell 41% to August 2019, including **−31% in the single month of the 2019 primary** ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). ARGT posted its best day on record after the October 2025 midterms ([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2025/10/27/business/argentina-stock-market-milei-party-win)) yet returned −9% from then to September 2026, against EEM's +21%, and the Merval trades at 19.8 times forward earnings against 13.4 for Brazil's Ibovespa because earnings growth has lagged ([Buenos Aires Times](https://www.batimes.com.ar/news/economy/argentine-stocks-are-left-behind-as-earnings-growth-eludes-milei.phtml)). Relative to EEM, Brazil underperformed by 43% over the three years after its October 2018 election, a period that included the 2019 pension reform; Chile's ETF trailed by 23% in the nine months after its December 2025 election; and after Vietnam's FTSE upgrade was announced, its ETF fell 11% from December 2025 to September 2026 while EEM rose 22% ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). Older reform booms ended the same way. New Zealand's share market fell almost 60% after its 1987 peak and "has never topped" that peak in capital terms ([NZ Herald](https://www.nzherald.co.nz/indepth/business/1987-stock-market-crash/)), and Mexico's market lost two-thirds of its dollar value between December 1994 and March 1995 ([Federal Reserve Bulletin](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/bulletin/1996/396lead.pdf)).

### Anti-market shocks hit within days, but feared policies often fail to arrive

Moves away from markets are rarely priced in advance, and they hit fast. China's 2020-2021 regulatory crackdown took the KWEB internet ETF down 79% peak to trough, and MCHI has trailed EEM by 55% since October 2020; Turkey's ETF trailed EEM by 44% over the three years after its turn to unorthodox monetary policy ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). MSCI removed Russia from its indices at "effectively zero" in March 2022 ([ETFdb](https://etfdb.com/emerging-markets-content-hub/msci-removes-russian-securities-from-em-indexes/)), and when Venezuela announced nationalisation of "strategic" sectors in January 2007 the Caracas index fell 19% ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2007/1/9/chavez-speech-hits-stock-values)). Fears are a different matter, because the feared policy often never arrives. Brazil's ETF fell 56% on 2002 election polls and then rose 203% by December 2003, Mexico recovered by the fifth year after its 2018 election, and Chile rallied when voters rejected a proposed new constitution ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)).

Markets also punish fiscal policy they see as unsound, whichever way it points. The UK's September 2022 package of £45bn in unfunded tax cuts was punished within two trading days (EWU −7.6%, against EFA −5.3%), and the relative loss was erased within five weeks of the reversal ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/10/21/3-charts-showing-the-market-turmoil-wreaked-during-uk-pm-truss-tenure.html)). The practical lesson is to treat enacted expropriation, capital controls or sanctions as a regime change rather than a dip, while recognising that a feared policy that is not yet law can be overpriced.

### Currency and concentration decide what a US investor actually earns

For a dollar investor, the exchange rate is often the biggest factor. Mexico's Bolsa fell about 39% in pesos but about two-thirds in dollars in 1994-95 ([Federal Reserve Bulletin](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/bulletin/1996/396lead.pdf)). Turkey's BIST-100 rose 35.6% in lira in 2023 while the TUR ETF lost 13% in dollars ([TR Monitor](https://www.trmonitor.net/insurance-was-the-star-of-borsa-istanbul-in-2023/)). From December 2020 to June 2024, unhedged Japan (EWJ) returned 7% while the yen-hedged DXJ returned 107%; DXJ tracks a different, exporter-tilted index, so not all of the gap is currency ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). Concentration matters too. ARGT tracks a 27-stock index in which MercadoLibre was 22.2% in September 2026, with YPF and Vista together another 17% ([TrackInsight](https://www.trackinsight.com/en/fund/ARGT)); because MercadoLibre is a US-listed company that earns most of its revenue outside Argentina, ARGT is partly a Latin American e-commerce bet.

### By October 2026 the re-rating has already happened in several reform stories

In the case studies, lasting winners shared three features: a cheap starting valuation, sustained macro stability, and earnings growth rather than just a higher multiple. Lasting losers had reform headlines without earnings delivery, as with Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 (−23% relative to EEM since April 2016) and Indonesia (−72% since July 2014) ([episodes.py](../research_notes/Economic%20freedom%20investment%20thesis/reform_case_studies_data/episodes.py)). On those criteria, several reform stories are already priced. From May 2019 to September 2026 it returned **about 178%, against 64% for EEM, 108.5% for the ex-China fund EMXC and 177% for SPY**, price-only ([Robinhood market data, 2026-10-16](https://robinhood.com/us/en/stocks/FRDM/)). The caveats are large. To December 2024 it was up only about 30% (EEM +3%, SPY +113%), so most of its lead came in 2025-2026 as Korea, Taiwan, Poland, Chile and Peru rallied; part of the edge comes from holding almost no China and little India; and it ran 23% annualised volatility against 18% for EEM. It is one seven-year sample, not a test. A democracy-weighted rival, DMCY, peaked at about $10m in assets and was liquidated in February 2026 ([SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1593547/000139834426001230/fp0097323-1_497.htm)).

## A small, rules-based, tax-aware tilt is the defensible implementation

### What the evidence supports and what it rejects

| Claim | Verdict | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Western tax burdens are rising | Partly supported | OECD record 34.1% of GDP; UK heading to 38%. But the OECD corporate rate stands at 24.1%, flat or falling, and Heritage tax-burden scores improved for the West (+0.5 vs −0.1) |
| Western economic freedom is falling | Weak, country-specific | West −0.13 vs rest −0.18 (2018-23), all in the COVID year; Canada 9th → 18th; 14 Western countries in Fraser's top third, none in the bottom |
| The West is de-industrialising | Supported for energy-intensive Europe and the UK; mixed for the US | German energy-intensive output −15.2%; 37 Mt of chemical closures; US factory construction −32% from its peak |
| Buying countries after measured freedom gains beats the market | Not supported | S1 −1.62%/yr (t −0.92); upper bound 1.3%/yr |
| Buying the freest countries beats the market | Not supported; freer markets are less volatile | S2 −1.51%/yr; Chen & Huang; Blau et al. |
| Countries cutting taxes outperform | Not supported | S3 −0.20%/yr; within developed markets −2.7%/yr (fragile) |
| Energy build-out predicts returns | Not supported | S5 −0.74%/yr |
| Manufacturing build-out predicts returns | Unproven, fragile hint | S4 +2.0%/yr (t 1.12); vanishes in ACWI-only and value-weighted variants |
| Freedom gains go with higher returns while they happen | Supported, but not tradable from index data | +3.65%/yr in the data year, +4.82% the year after, +0.68% in the publication year |
| Avoiding capital controls and expropriation protects returns | Plausible, not testable in the backtest | Russia marked −100%; KWEB −79%; Russia is the only collapse in the sample |
| Country momentum predicts returns | Supported in the repository's test; fragile in the literature | Fama-MacBeth t about 3; in a large robustness study, only short-term momentum survives |

### Five ways to act, with their trade-offs

| Approach | What it does | Evidence for | Main costs and risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global market-cap index, no tilt | Lets prices absorb policy news | No signal beat the market once publication lags were respected; Norway's sovereign fund declined growth tilts for the same reason ([NBIM](https://www.nbim.no/en/publications/discussion-notes/2012/economic-growth-and-equity-returns)) | Holds whatever the index holds, including countries moving away |
| Ready-made freedom-weighted EM fund (FRDM), or the cheaper ex-China route (EMXC) | Weights emerging markets by freedom scores, or simply drops China | FRDM +178% vs EEM +64% since May 2019, but mostly in 2025-26 and partly an ex-China effect | FRDM costs 0.49% vs 0.25% for EMXC; volatility 23% vs 18%; one sample; DMCY's closure shows niche funds can disappear |
| Rules-based country sleeve | Uses policy direction as a filter, 12-month momentum and valuation as rankers, volatility-scaled weights, annual rebalancing and caps | Momentum was the only robust predictor (t about 3); buying the cheapest third of countries by CAPE beat equal weighting by 4-7%/yr gross in 1980-2011 | Single-country fees of 0.50-0.75%; wide spreads; rebalancing realises gains; value and momentum have decayed |
| Event-driven reform trades | Buys on credible early signals (primaries, enacted laws) and reviews after 12-24 months | Run-ups before events and first-year premia in about two-thirds of cases; Henry 2000 | Binary election risk (Argentina −31% in August 2019); reversals by years 3-5; requires forecasting |
| Thematic energy and industrial ETFs | Funds such as GRID, PAVE, XLI, URA and XLE | No study links build-out to country returns; S5 −0.74%/yr | Behave like US stocks (correlation 0.75-0.87 with SPY); URA's historical drawdown was −93% |

Size the tilt to the plausible edge, which is small. The repository's long-only tilts produced −0.7% to +1.0% a year before costs, while single-country ETFs charge about 0.50-0.75% a year against 0.25% for EMXC ([ETFValuer](https://etfvaluer.com/etf/EMXC)). Single-country emerging-market ETFs ran 17-34% annualised volatility over the past decade, against 15.6% for SPY, and since listing the Brazil, Turkey, Greece, Chile, Poland and Korea funds have suffered peak-to-trough losses of 70-80% ([Robinhood market data, 2026-10-16](https://robinhood.com/us/en/stocks/EWZ/)). In this report's judgment, those numbers argue for a sleeve of roughly 5-15% of equities, per-country caps of about 2-5% of equities (lower for the most volatile and least liquid funds), and volatility-scaled weights, under which a 30%-volatility fund such as EWZ or ARGT gets about half the dollar weight of a 15%-volatility fund such as EWJ or EWL for the same risk. At a 10% sleeve, even a full 1%-a-year edge adds only about 0.1% a year to the whole portfolio, so the main reason to hold such a tilt is diversification that matches the investor's views, not expected outperformance.

A rule set consistent with the evidence would first use policy direction as a filter rather than a ranking, excluding countries with active capital controls, recent expropriation, sanctions exposure, an open index-exclusion consultation or a measured move away from markets. It would then rank the survivors on 12-month momentum, the one robust predictor in the repository's test, and on valuation. Buying the cheapest third of countries by CAPE beat equal weighting by 4-7% a year gross over 1980-2011 ([CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=21856)), although country value and reversal premia have decayed since about 2000 ([Zaremba et al.](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/output/3010882/where-have-the-profits-gone-market-efficiency-and-the-disappearing-equity-anomalies-in-country-and-industry-returns)) and a 2025 study testing thousands of design variants finds most country signals fragile, with only market size, dividend yield, short-term momentum and sovereign risk surviving ([Cakici et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5181455)). Finally, it would rebalance once a year within tolerance bands, because country momentum persists for about a year and reverses over the following two ([Bhojraj](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ucp/jnlbus/v79y2006i1p429-428.html)). Reform-event entries belong in a separate, smaller satellite, reviewed after 12-24 months or after euphoric index events such as upgrades; that review rule is a heuristic drawn from the case studies, not a tested result. Applied in October 2026, such a rule would hold few of the policy leaders. Argentina, Chile and Vietnam fail the momentum screen, the momentum leaders (Korea, Taiwan, Poland, Peru) are the markets that have already re-rated, and Brazil is cheap and rising but faces a binary runoff on 25 October.

### Tax mechanics decide how much of any edge survives

For a US taxable account, fund domicile comes first. Foreign-domiciled funds, such as European UCITS ETFs, are generally passive foreign investment companies (PFICs), whose gains are taxed by default at the top ordinary rate, spread back over the holding period with an interest charge, and each fund needs a Form 8621 every year; only US-domiciled ETFs and US-listed ADRs make sense ([HCVT](https://www.hcvt.com/alertarticle-PFIC-Reporting-for-Direct-and-Indirect-Investors)). Foreign withholding tax can be recovered through the foreign tax credit. A US fund with more than half its assets in foreign stock can elect under Section 853 to pass foreign withholding through to shareholders, who see it in Box 7 of Form 1099-DIV and can claim the credit ([26 USC §853](https://policyrisk.com/us-code/title/26/section/853)), and Form 1116 can be skipped if passive foreign taxes are $300 or less, or $600 on a joint return ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1116)). Withholding suffered inside an IRA cannot be credited, so funds from high-withholding countries belong in the taxable account.

Qualified-dividend status varies sharply between funds that look alike. In 2025 iShares reported **100% of dividends qualified for Mexico (EWW), 91% for Poland (EPOL), 58% for Korea (EWY), 56% for Japan (EWJ), and 0% for Brazil (EWZ), India (INDA), Saudi Arabia (KSA) and Taiwan (EWT)** ([iShares 2025 QDI summary](https://www.ishares.com/us/literature/tax-information/2025-ishares-qdi-summary-stamped.pdf)). EWZ's roughly 3.5% yield is therefore taxed at ordinary rates while EWW's 4.0% is not. ADRs of the same companies can still qualify, because shares readily tradable on a US exchange meet the qualified-dividend test, and IRS Notice 2024-11 added Chile to the list of qualifying treaty countries while removing Russia and Hungary ([IRS Notice 2024-11](https://www.eitc.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-11.pdf)). Brazil's new 10% dividend withholding adds a drag from 2026 that the investor recovers only through the foreign tax credit ([EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2025-2232)). Currency-hedged funds can make unplanned taxable distributions, as when HEWJ paid about $12 on a roughly $27 share in December 2022 ([Digrin](https://www.digrin.com/stocks/detail/HEWJ/)). Annual rebalancing realises capital gains, and directing new contributions to underweight positions reduces how much has to be sold. Country withholding rates were not verified in this research and should be checked in each fund's tax documents.

### Tail risks the backtest could not measure

The backtest could not measure the risk the thesis is best placed to avoid, and the precedents are severe. iShares paused creations in its Russia ETF (ERUS) on 1 March 2022, VanEck's RSX was halted and delisted on 4 March, and RSX holders received liquidation payments in instalments from January 2023 through December 2024 ([etf.com](https://www.etf.com/sections/news/ishares-halts-new-creations-erus); [VanEck](https://www.vaneck.com/Funds/RSX.aspx)). MSCI's standalone decision on Indonesia is due by 30 June 2027, with estimated forced selling by index funds of $3-19bn ([The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/business/business-news/2026/10/16/jakarta-stocks-plunge-11-as-msci-set-to-drop-indonesia-from-indexes)). Argentina still has some capital controls and an MSCI standalone rating, Mexico has put its energy sector back under state control, and the Gulf's energy leg turned negative in 2026, with Qatar's gas output down about 67% in March-July ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/13/middle-east-and-central-asias-economy-to-shrink-in-2026-amid-war-imf-says)). Elections are binary events for reform trades. Argentina's ETF lost 31% in the month of its 2019 primary, Brazil votes on 25 October 2026, and on 7 October 2026 a prediction market gave Argentina's incumbent a 55.5% chance in the 2027 presidential election ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/argentina-markets-merval-peso-wednesday-october-7-2026/)). Position caps, not hedges, are the practical defence, together with a firm rule not to enter, or to exit, markets that impose capital controls, expropriate foreign holders or face sanctions. Currency hedging is cheap only where local interest rates sit below US rates, as in Japan, where hedged DXJ fell 5% from December 2021 to October 2022 while unhedged EWJ fell 27% ([Robinhood market data, 2026-10-16](https://robinhood.com/us/en/stocks/DXJ/)).

## Conclusion

The thesis holds up as a description of policy divergence but fails as a rule for picking countries from published data. The repository's timing test shows why both are true: freedom gains earn roughly 4-5% a year while they happen and almost nothing once the index reports them, so the information is real but belongs to whoever forecasts the change first, and the early signals that carry it (primaries, polls, bills passing) are also where the binary election risk sits. Two implications follow that the original framing misses. The evidence is strongest on the defensive side, because freer markets are less volatile and the catastrophic losses in the record (Russia, Venezuela, China's crackdown, Indonesia now) cluster in countries moving away from markets; the thesis therefore works better as an exclusion screen than as a source of extra return. And in October 2026 policy and price point in opposite directions: the policy leaders are the year's laggards, the momentum leaders have already re-rated, and the UK, the Western economy that best fits the thesis's worries, is among the cheapest developed markets, so a rule that respects momentum, the one robust predictor in the test, would currently hold few of the countries the thesis favours.

Two kinds of new evidence would change these conclusions. One is a point-in-time policy signal that leads the indices, such as tax and capital-account changes coded on the day they are enacted, and that survives momentum controls out of sample. The other is Western policy change in 2025-2026 large enough to resemble the discrete regime changes markets have repriced in the past, rather than the marginal shifts the data show so far. Until then, the defensible position is a small, capped, tax-efficient tilt whose main job is to keep the investor away from regime breaks, with any conviction about a specific reform acted on early, sized small and reviewed within two years.
