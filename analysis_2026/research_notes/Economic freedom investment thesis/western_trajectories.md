# Western Policy Trajectories 2015 to October 2026: Taxes, Regulation, Economic Freedom, Energy, Industry

Research date: 16 October 2026. Scope: US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Netherlands, Nordics, Switzerland, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, EU bloc. The premise tested: "Western countries are moving toward higher taxes, de-industrialization and less economic freedom."

Method notes for the report writer:
- Direct page fetching (WebFetch/curl) was blocked by the session's network proxy for every data host tried (fraserinstitute.org, heritage.org, worldbank, eurostat, OECD SDMX, EIA, FRED). All findings below come from web-search result summaries of the cited pages. Figures were cross-checked across two or more results where possible; single-source or secondary-aggregator figures are flagged "(secondary)" or "[UNVERIFIED]".
- The session-wide web search budget ran out before every planned query was finished. Gaps from that are listed under each question.
- Fraser ranks are revised between editions (Canada's 2023 rank was 11th in the 2025 edition and 9th as revised in the 2026 edition), so cross-edition rank changes are approximate.
- Heritage and Fraser are both free-market think tanks. They use different methods and, for the US in 2025, point in opposite directions (see Q1).

## Q1. Economic freedom scores and ranks over time (Fraser EFW and Heritage Index)

### Takeaway
On the two main indices, most Western countries are flat or improving, not falling. The clearest decliners are Canada (Fraser: about 11th to 18th in one year), Germany (Fraser 15th to 19th) and the US on Heritage over 2020-2025. France, the UK and Switzerland improved on Fraser in the latest edition. The global average is still below its 2019 level. For the US, the indices disagree about 2025: Heritage's 2026 edition shows the biggest US gain since 2001, while Fraser's preliminary 2025 estimate shows a US decline driven by trade restrictions and bigger government.

### Inferences
- **Fraser rank direction, about 2015 data to 2024 data** (approximate across editions):
  - US: 11th to 5th, improved.
  - Canada: 11th to 18th, deteriorated, almost all of it in 2023-2024.
  - UK: about 10th, then 13th, then 10th, roughly flat.
  - Germany: 23rd, then 15th, then 19th. Up over the decade, down in the latest year.
  - France: 52nd, then 44th, then 39th, steadily improving.
  - Italy: 46th to 47th, flat.
  - Switzerland: 4th to 2nd.
  - New Zealand: 3rd to 4th.
  - Ireland: 6th to 6th.
  - Denmark: 9th to 7th.
  - Australia: 7th (tied) to 8th.
  - Netherlands: 10th to 10th (tied).
- On Fraser, the premise ("less economic freedom") holds clearly only for Canada and, in the latest year, Germany. France, the UK in the latest year, Switzerland and Denmark moved the other way.
- Heritage shows a long US decline over 2020-2025 and a rebound in the 2026 edition. Fraser's preliminary 2025 estimate shows a US decline. The difference is plausibly about weighting and timing: tariffs fall under Fraser's trade area and partly outside Heritage's coverage window. Treat US direction in 2025 as contested between the indices.
- The Nordics, the Netherlands, Ireland and Switzerland score above the US on Heritage 2026 and close to it on Fraser. That works against a blanket "Western Europe is unfree" framing. These are high-tax states that score well on rule of law, trade and regulation.

### Gaps
- Exact Fraser EFW 2026 scores (not just ranks) for most countries. Ranks for Sweden, Norway, Spain and Iceland. Fraser's 1 to 10 score history 2015-2024 per country. The proxy blocked direct access to efotw.org and the report PDF.
- Heritage 2026 official scores for France, Italy, Spain, Australia and New Zealand (only secondary figures found). Heritage's data cutoff for the 2026 edition, and so whether the April 2025 US tariffs are reflected, was not confirmed.
- Heritage 2015 baseline scores per country were not retrieved.

## Q2. Tax burden: revenue/GDP, rates, recent budgets, government spending

### Takeaway
The OECD-wide tax take hit a record 34.1% of GDP in 2024, and the UK, France and Germany raised or extended taxes in 2024-2026. That fits the premise. But the US, Canada, Germany (for companies, from 2028), Italy (middle incomes), the Nordics (corporate and top-rate cuts planned or enacted), New Zealand and Switzerland (voters rejected a 50% inheritance tax by 78%) cut or blocked taxes. France's tax ratio is actually slightly lower than in 2000. The picture is split by country, not a uniform Western rise.

### Cited Findings

**Aggregate tax-to-GDP (OECD Revenue Statistics 2025, published December 2025; 2024 figures preliminary)**
- The OECD average was 34.1% of GDP in 2024, up 0.3 percentage points from 2023 and the highest in the series. Of 36 countries reporting 2024 data, 22 rose, 13 fell and 1 was flat. Labour taxes, especially personal income tax, drove the increase. — [OECD press release, Dec 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/12/labour-taxes-drive-oecd-tax-revenues-to-record-high-in-2024.html); [OECD Revenue Statistics 2025, trends chapter](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025_07ca0a8e/full-report/tax-revenue-trends-1965-2024_98c75833.html)
- 2024 by country:
  - Denmark 45.2%, the highest in the OECD for the 2nd year.
  - France 43.5%, 2nd. It was 43.7% in 2000, so slightly lower now.
  - Austria 43.4%.
  - Netherlands 38.5%, down from 39.3% in 2023, rank 11th.
  - Germany 38.0%, up from 37.3% in 2023, rank 13th.
  - US 25.6%, unchanged, 31st of 38.
  - Ireland fell from 27.7% in 2010 to 21.7% in 2024, the largest fall in the OECD.
  - Norway fell 1.4 points in 2024.
  - Mexico 18.3% was the lowest.
  - Sources: [OECD France country note (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025-country-notes_3708be73/france_77750c88/c55b0aeb-en.pdf); [OECD US country note (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025-country-notes_3708be73/united-states_f45a114c/efcc8c03-en.pdf); [OECD Germany note (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025-country-notes_3708be73/germany_bb52e7ad/4551991c-en.pdf); [OECD Netherlands note (PDF)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025-country-notes_3708be73/netherlands_a8c76e8e/abde048b-en.pdf); [OECD trends chapter](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/12/revenue-statistics-2025_07ca0a8e/full-report/tax-revenue-trends-1965-2024_98c75833.html)

**Corporate tax rates**
- The average OECD member statutory corporate rate fell from 32.3% in 2000 to 24.1% in 2025. The combined average for Inclusive Framework countries was 21.2% in 2025 versus 21.1% in 2024: stable, after falling from 28.0% in 2000 to 21.7% in 2019. — [OECD Corporate Tax Statistics press release, Nov 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/corporate-tax-revenues-rise-as-global-corporate-tax-rates-continue-to-stabilise-new-oecd-data-show.html)
- Tax Foundation 2025: OECD average top corporate rate 24.20%. Worldwide average 23.58% (26.04% GDP-weighted), down from 40.18% in 1980 and "leveled off in recent years". Czechia raised its rate (19% to 21%) and Portugal cut (31.5% to 30.5%) in 2025. — [Tax Foundation, Corporate Tax Rates Around the World 2025](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/global/corporate-tax-rates-by-country-2025/)

**United States: One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA)**
- Signed 4 July 2025. CBO estimates it adds $3.4 trillion to deficits over 2025-2034, or $4.1 trillion with interest. Its tax changes total about $4.4 trillion over ten years, mostly by permanently extending and expanding the 2017 TCJA provisions. — [Peterson Foundation](https://www.pgpf.org/article/how-did-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-change-tax-policy/); [CRFB](https://www.crfb.org/blogs/whats-one-big-beautiful-bill-act)
- Corporate rate unchanged at 21%. 100% bonus depreciation made permanent for property acquired after 19 January 2025. Domestic R&E expensing restored. Section 179 limit raised to $2.5 million. — [Butler Snow](https://www.butlersnow.com/news-and-events/the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act-and-its-potential-business-impact); [MFS](https://www.mfs.com/en-au/investment-professional/insights/market-insights/one-big-beautiful-bill-highlights-tax-cuts-investment-incentives.html)
- SALT cap raised to $40,000 for filers under $500,000 income, indexed 1% a year for 2025-2029, then back to $10,000. — [Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/blog/one-big-beautiful-bill-pros-cons.)
- Wind and solar production and investment credits (45Y/48E) end for projects placed in service after 31 December 2027, unless construction began before 4 July 2026. — [Simpson Thacher](https://www.stblaw.com/about-us/publications/view/2025/07/08/president-trump-signs-legislation-enacting-phased-elimination-of-federal-tax-credits-for-new-clean-energy-projects-directs-treasury-to-strictly-enforce-credit-termination-for-solar-and-wind-facilities); [IRS Notice 2025-42](https://www.eitc.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-42.pdf)

**United Kingdom**
- Autumn Budget, 30 October 2024, about £40bn of tax rises:
  - Employer National Insurance rose from 13.8% to 15% from April 2025, and the employer threshold fell from £9,100 to £5,000. The Employment Allowance rose to £10,500.
  - Capital gains tax rates rose from 10% to 18% (lower) and from 20% to 24% (higher).
  - The non-dom remittance basis was abolished from 6 April 2025 and replaced by a 4-year foreign income and gains regime.
  - Carried interest is taxed at about 31-32%; sources differ.
  - Sources: [Portfolio Adviser](https://adv.portfolio-adviser.com/?p=306428); [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/uk-autumn-budget-2024-what-uk-business-tax-measures-were-announced); [RSM UK](https://www.rsmuk.com/insights/budget/previous-budget-announcements/autumn-budget-2024-highlights); [Gravita](https://www.gravita.com/gravitas-autumn-budget-highlights-2024)
- Budget, 26 November 2025: about £26bn of tax rises by 2029-30.
  - Income tax thresholds stay frozen at £12,570, £50,270 and £125,140 until 2030-31 (the freeze was due to end in 2028).
  - The OBR projects the tax take reaching an all-time high of 38% of GDP in 2030-31.
  - Fiscal drag adds 5.2m income-tax payers between 2022-23 and 2030-31. The higher- or additional-rate share of taxpayers rises from 15% (2021-22) to 24% (2030-31).
  - New annual surcharge of £2,500 on homes over £2m, rising to £7,500 over £5m.
  - Higher taxes on property, savings and dividend income.
  - Sources: [Mortgage Solutions](https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/news/2025/11/26/autumn-budget-2025-millions-face-fiscal-drag-as-reeves-freezes-income-tax-thresholds/); [Accountingweb](https://www.accountingweb.co.uk/node/227808); [UK Property Accountants](https://www.ukpropertyaccountants.co.uk/autumn-budget-update-4-income-tax-threshold-freeze-mansion-tax-everything-in-between/); [Workplace Journal](https://workplacejournal.co.uk/2025/11/autumn-budget-2025-extended-tax-threshold-freeze-will-pull-millions-into-higher-bands-aegon-warns/)
- North Sea Energy Profits Levy kept to 31 March 2030 (headline combined rate up to 78%), then replaced by an "Oil and Gas Price Mechanism". — [Holyrood](https://www.holyrood.com/news/view,uk-government-to-overhaul-north-sea-tax-regime-after-energy-profits-levy-ends-in-2030); [Baird Maritime](https://www.bairdmaritime.com/offshore/drilling-production/uk-permits-new-oil-and-gas-drilling-but-keeps-windfall-tax-until-2030)

**France**
- The 2025 Finance Act introduced a "one-off" surtax on the largest companies (about €8bn) and a temporary minimum tax of 20% on high incomes.
- The 2026 budget was adopted on 2 February 2026 after PM Lecornu used Article 49.3 twice; the Constitutional Council validated it on 19 February 2026.
  - The large-company surtax was extended one more year. Sources differ on rates: 20.6% for turnover above €1.5bn, or two tiers of 20.6% and 41.2% with the threshold raised from €1bn to €1.5bn.
  - KPMG says the high-income minimum tax is extended until the deficit falls below 3% of GDP. Blevins Franks describes the 3%/4% surcharge above €250k/€500k as unchanged. The two descriptions are not reconciled.
  - The CVAE production tax is still being abolished by 2030.
  - The deficit target is 5% of GDP for 2026.
- Sources: [ING Think](https://think.ing.com/articles/frances-2026-budget-likely-to-pass/); [KPMG Avocats, 5 Feb 2026](https://kpmg.com/av/fr/avocats/eclairages/2026/02/tax-measures-in-finance-act-2026-adopted-in-france.html); [Blevins Franks](https://www.blevinsfranks.com/frances-2026-budget-and-taxation/); [Qualifisc](https://qualifisc.com/en/tax-updates-insights/a-hard-won-2026-budget-the-key-takeaways/); [Ecostylia](https://www.ecostylia.com/en/budget-49-3-france/)

**Germany**
- Constitutional debt-brake reform and €500bn 12-year infrastructure fund, signed 22 March 2025. The fund splits into €100bn for the Länder, €100bn for the Climate and Transformation Fund and €300bn for federal infrastructure. Defence spending above 1% of GDP is exempt from the debt brake, and the Länder may borrow 0.35% of GDP. — [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/energy/2025-posts/germanys-ambitious-fiscal-reforms-what-germanys-500b-plan-could-mean-for-infrastructure-investors); [Social Europe](https://www.socialeurope.eu/germany-ditches-debt-brake-a-fiscal-revolution-begins)
- "Immediate tax investment programme", passed by the Bundestag in late June 2025, with the Bundesrat vote on 11 July 2025 described as a formality:
  - Declining-balance depreciation of up to 30% for movable assets acquired from 1 July 2025 to the end of 2027.
  - Corporate income tax cut from 15% to 10% in 1-point steps over 2028-2032.
  - The Länder forecast €28bn of lost revenue for 2025-2029.
  - Sources: [PwC Germany](https://blogs.pwc.de/en/german-tax-and-legal-news/article/249616/act-for-an-immediate-tax-investment-programme-to-strengthen-germany-as-a-business-location/); [KPMG Germany](https://kpmg.com/de/en/home/insights/2025/07/federal-council-votes-tax-rate-amendment-to.html); [OSW](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/analyses/2025-07-04/bundestag-adopts-a-tax-relief-package-business); [The Local](https://www.thelocal.de/20250604/germany-unveils-tax-breaks-to-boost-stagnant-economy)

**Canada**
- On 21 March 2025 the government dropped the planned rise in the capital-gains inclusion rate from one-half to two-thirds. Budget 2025 confirmed this. — [KPMG Canada](https://kpmg.com/ca/en/home/insights/2025/09/canadian-federal-budget-2025.html); [ATB](https://www.atb.com/wealth/good-advice/tax/federal-budget-2025/)
- Budget 2025 (November 2025):
  - "Productivity super-deduction", including a 100% first-year write-off for manufacturing and processing machinery. The government projects it lowers the corporate marginal effective tax rate from 15.6% to 13.2%, which it calls the lowest in the G7.
  - The lowest personal income tax rate falls from 15% to 14.5% for 2025 and to 14% from 2026.
  - Sources: [ATB](https://www.atb.com/wealth/good-advice/tax/federal-budget-2025/); [Mintz](https://insights.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2906/2025-11-06-canadas-federal-budget-2025-cost-playing-it-safe)

**Italy**

  - Corporate rate cut from 20% to 18% from tax year 2027; one source still calls it "proposed".

## Q3. Regulation and trade: deregulation, EU burden and simplification, US tariffs

### Takeaway
On regulation, the 2025-2026 direction is mostly **lighter**: US federal deregulation, the EU's Omnibus I cut CSRD/CSDDD scope by about 90%, and the AI Act's high-risk rules were delayed 16-24 months. On trade, the US moved sharply **toward protection**: its effective tariff rate peaked around 16-17% (highest since 1936) before the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026. Section 122, 301 and 232 tariffs then rebuilt part of the wall. In Fraser's terms the trade change lowers US economic freedom even where it is framed as pro-industry.

### Cited Findings

**US deregulation**
- Executive Order 14192 (31 January 2025) requires agencies to repeal at least 10 rules for each new one. The OIRA FY2025 tally is 646 deregulatory actions against 5 significant regulatory actions. **US tariffs, 2025-2026**
- Yale Budget Lab:
  - Before the ruling, the average effective tariff rate (AETR) was 16.0% (later cited as 16.9% had the IEEPA tariffs stayed), the highest since 1936.

## Q4. Energy: industrial electricity and gas prices, nuclear, oil and gas production, grids

### Takeaway
The cost gap between Europe and the US is the strongest data point for the "de-industrialization" premise in Europe. EU wholesale power cost about 2x the US in 2025, German power about 2.4x since 2022, and European gas 2.5-5x US gas. The UK has the highest industrial power prices in the IEA. The 2026 Strait of Hormuz shock pushed European gas to a 4-year high (TTF about €86/MWh in mid-October 2026). Meanwhile US LNG exports set records. Policy is changing: Belgium repealed its nuclear phase-out, Italy is moving to un-ban nuclear, and Germany and the UK introduced industrial power subsidies for 2026-2027. The UK still bans new North Sea exploration licences. US power prices are also rising (up about 25.7% for residential since 2022).

### Inferences
- **The de-industrialization premise is strongest for Germany, the UK and the energy-intensive EU (chemicals, steel, refining).** The data shows lasting capacity removal, not just a cyclical dip, and investment collapsing alongside closures.
- **The US is mixed.** The 2021-2024 CHIPS/IRA factory-building boom is unwinding. In 2025 the US cut clean-energy manufacturing credits, raised input tariffs and saw factory employment fall. Tariffs framed as pro-industry coincided with manufacturing contraction in 2025 (correlation, not proven causation). Energy and tax advantages (100% expensing, cheap gas) remain.
- Manufacturing's share of GDP has been falling across all rich economies for decades (US 16.1% in 1997 to about 10% in 2024; UK 8.0% in 2024). The 2015-2026 change is a continuation of a long trend, with a sharper break in European energy-intensive sectors after 2022.

## Q6. Where the premise is wrong or mixed, and a country-by-country scorecard

### Takeaway
The premise ("the West is uniformly raising taxes, de-industrializing and losing economic freedom") is **too broad**. The data supports a narrower claim: **Canada, the UK on taxes, Germany on industry, and France on tax level** fit parts of it. The **US** cut taxes and deregulated but raised tariffs and deficits. **Switzerland, Ireland, the Nordics, the Netherlands, Australia, New Zealand and Italy** are stable or moving toward lower taxes or more freedom on at least one major measure. The best-supported broad trend is **European energy-intensive de-industrialization**, driven mainly by energy costs (49% of chemical closures cite energy; 8% cite regulation).

### Inferences
- **Contradicting evidence, strongest items:**
  1. France, Switzerland and Denmark improved their Fraser ranks; the US improved from 11th to 5th over the decade.
  2. Corporate tax rates are flat or falling across the OECD, with explicit cuts in Germany, Finland, Sweden and Denmark (planned) and investment-expensing in the US, Canada and New Zealand.
  3. The EU and US are both deregulating in 2025-2026.
  4. Swiss voters rejected a 50% inheritance tax by 78%.
  5. The tax ratio fell in 2024 in the Netherlands, Norway and Ireland.
- **Supporting evidence, strongest items:**
  1. Record OECD tax-to-GDP (34.1%), with the UK heading to a record 38%.
  2. Canada's sharp Fraser drop.
  3. Germany's and the EU's energy-intensive industrial contraction, and UK heavy-industry closures.
  4. Rising government spending (Germany to 49.4%, France at 57.2%) and deficits (US +$3.4T).
  5. US tariffs at a 90-year high, which lower trade freedom.
  6. Tightening taxes on mobile wealth (UK non-doms, Italy's flat tax, Norway's exit tax, France's minimum tax).
- **Better framing for an investment thesis:** divergence within the West. Small open economies (Switzerland, Ireland, the Nordics, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Australia) keep high and stable freedom scores. Large economies with fiscal stress (UK, France) raise taxes. Energy-importing industrial economies (Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, the UK) shed energy-intensive industry. The US combines low taxes and deregulation with protectionism and large deficits.
- Measurement caution: Heritage and Fraser disagree on the US in 2025. Heritage's 2026 edition likely only partly reflects 2025 tariffs; Fraser's full 2025 data arrives in the 2027 edition. Any backtest of "freedom score changes leading to market returns" should use the publication date, not the data year, to avoid look-ahead bias.
